## For each document, answer two questions

### 1. Is this narrative, or is it reference material?

**narrative** — the document enacts the story. Characters speak and act in it. Events happen
on the page.

**reference** — the document describes the work from outside it. A cast list, a character
bible, an outline, a synopsis, notes to the author's future self, a critical essay about the
work, front matter, an editor's introduction.

The test is whether relationships in the document are *shown happening* or *stated about*. A
scene in which two characters argue is narrative. A page saying "these two are estranged
siblings" is reference, even if it is beautifully written, and even if it sits in the same
folder as the chapters.

Answer `unsure` if the document does not clearly do either. That is a real answer and it is
better than a confident wrong one; a person will be shown what you said and can correct it.

### 2. Where does the narrative begin and end?

Some documents contain more than one kind of material bound together — an editor's preface
before the novel starts, an appendix after it ends, a transcriber's note, a title page.

If the whole document is one kind of material, say so and give no boundaries.

If it is not, give the text that the narrative **begins with** and the text it **ends with**:
a short verbatim quotation from the document, long enough to be found unambiguously. Do not
paraphrase, do not summarise, and do not correct spelling or punctuation. The quotation must
appear in the document exactly as you give it, because it will be searched for literally.

**Do not invent a boundary you are unsure of.** A document reported as one continuous piece
of narrative is easy for a person to correct. A boundary placed in the middle of a chapter
silently removes that chapter from the analysis, and nothing on screen will say why.
